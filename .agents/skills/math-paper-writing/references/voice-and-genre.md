# Voice, genre, and audience

Read this reference when the request changes the kind of paper, the expected reader, the level of exposition, or the researcher's personal voice.

## Choose the genre before optimizing prose

Different mathematical documents make different promises:

| Genre | Primary obligation | Typical compression |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Full research paper | Establish and contextualize a substantial result | Definitions and proof interfaces remain explicit |
| Short note | Deliver one focused result or correction | Remove peripheral background, not necessary hypotheses |
| Survey or exposition | Build an accurate map of known ideas for a stated audience | Explain connections; distinguish cited results from new synthesis |
| Computational paper | Connect mathematical claims to reproducible algorithms or experiments | State data, parameters, error, and reproducibility contracts |
| Internal review draft | Enable verification and expose uncertainty | May include ledgers and status warnings excluded from publication prose |
| Submission correspondence | Help an editor or referee evaluate a specific manuscript | Concise, factual, and synchronized with the submitted snapshot |

Do not compress a paper by deleting the information needed to state or verify its result. Do not make a research paper read like a teaching chapter unless the audience and venue call for that level of exposition.

## Build an audience contract

Name a plausible reader rather than using labels such as "general audience" without content. Record:

- concepts the reader can be expected to know;
- definitions, examples, and references the paper must supply;
- the first likely jump in difficulty;
- which details can be deferred without creating a backward dependency;
- what the reader should retain from each main section.

For an interdisciplinary audience, define terms whose names are familiar but whose normalization or meaning varies by field. For specialists, concise standard definitions may replace introductory examples, but nonstandard conventions still require explanation.

## Preserve or learn the researcher's voice

Use an existing paper or passage as a voice sample only when it is supplied by the researcher, belongs to the researcher, or is explicitly approved as a model. Prefer a sample from the same language and genre.

Extract a compact profile of decisions rather than phrases:

| Dimension | Examples of a usable observation |
| --- | --- |
| Register | formal, conversational in motivation, or uniformly neutral |
| Agency | preference for `we`, passive constructions, or named objects as subjects |
| Density | amount of explanation between displays and formal statements |
| Navigation | frequency and form of proof strategy and section previews |
| Rhythm | typical sentence and paragraph length, without copying sentences |
| Terminology | stable field-specific words and distinctions |
| Restraint | treatment of significance, novelty, limitations, and open questions |

Apply the profile only where it does not conflict with mathematics, venue rules, or clarity. Preserve deliberate habits, but do not preserve ambiguity, unsupported claims, broken grammar, or inconsistent notation merely because they occur in the sample.

If no approved sample exists, use a neutral, direct mathematical register. Do not claim to reproduce the researcher's personal voice from a few isolated sentences.

## Learning from other mathematicians

Use published mathematicians' advice and papers to learn abstract decisions such as audience control, example placement, proof navigation, notation economy, and expository restraint. Do not imitate distinctive wording, rhetorical signatures, jokes, anecdotes, or recognizable passages.

When the researcher asks for writing "like" a named mathematician, translate the request into high-level traits, state those traits, and apply only the traits that fit the present paper. The result should remain the researcher's paper, not a simulation of another author's voice.

## Cross-genre adaptation

When adapting one document into another, create a content contract before rewriting:

- what claims and evidence status must remain;
- what context the new audience lacks;
- what detail can be summarized and where the full argument remains available;
- which citations, figures, notation, and definitions must travel with the claim;
- which assertions are inappropriate for the new genre.

A cover letter cannot become a substitute for the abstract. A survey cannot silently present cited results as new. A short note cannot hide omitted proof steps behind space constraints. A Chinese review version may explain more, but it must not fork the mathematics.

## Voice validation

After revision, read representative passages from the beginning, middle, and end. Check that:

1. the voice profile is visible without repeated stock phrases;
2. formal claims remain distinguishable from motivation and opinion;
3. explanation density matches the audience throughout;
4. terminology and grammatical agency remain consistent;
5. no recognizable wording has been copied from a model source;
6. mathematical meaning and evidence status are unchanged.
